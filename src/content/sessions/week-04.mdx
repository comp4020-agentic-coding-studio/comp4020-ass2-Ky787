Work it through, because the semester's central habit starts here — do the
arithmetic yourself before believing a tool about it.

`%6` is `~(x ^ 0x01010101)`. `%14` is `(~x) ^ 0x01010101`, which is *the same
value*: complement and XOR commute. So `%7 = 42 · %6` and `%15 = −42 · %6`
cancel exactly. `%9` is `1 · x`. What is left is a constant fold:

```text
−1128481603  +  1145324680  −  68   =   16843009   =   0x01010101
```

The expression is `x + 0x01010101`, spelled in nine operations, and every
choice in it — the 42, the split constants, the pointless multiply by one — is
noise placed to make the cancellation non-obvious.

Now the machine code the same expression became:

The backend kept all of it. `imul eax, eax, 0x2a` is the multiply by 42;
`shl edx, 0x0` is the multiply by one, lowered to a shift by zero and then not
removed. This is the opposite of last week's result: at `-O0` the arithmetic
survived instruction selection completely.

:::warning[Bit width is part of the semantics]
Every value here is `uint32`, so every identity is an identity *modulo 2³²*.
`−1128481603 + 1145324680 − 68` is a 32-bit wraparound computation. An
identity you check over the integers can be false over 32-bit values, and one
that is false over the integers can be true over them. Never drop the width
when you write the obligation down.
:::

## What a simplifier is actually claiming

A simplification workflow has three expressions, not two: the **input** lifted
from the machine code, the **candidate** the tool proposes, and — only in a
controlled study like this one — the **expected** expression from the known
source. Two separate equivalence obligations follow, and they answer different
questions.

  <Query

Read that carefully. The candidate *was* the right answer — the
candidate-to-expected query is UNSAT. The soundness query hit its ten-second
budget and returned `unknown`. So the recovery is **not accepted**, and it is
recorded as `SMT UNKNOWN`, not as a success with an asterisk.

<Badge tone="partial">UNKNOWN</Badge> is not a weak yes. It is the absence of
an answer. A workflow that treats it as a yes is a workflow that will one day
accept something false.

The first two numbers are the good news, and they are about *isolated sites*:
one operation, one variable, a known shape. The second two are what happens
when the operations feed each other.

A second frozen probe was built for exactly this — four small branchless
`uint32` functions of four to ten operations, where intermediates are consumed
by later operations. Machine expressions were extracted for 44 of 48
build/function cases; the four that failed hit a preregistered AST size guard
on carry-flag expressions and never reached the simplifier, so they are
extraction limits and not simplifier failures. Of the twenty obfuscated
complete return expressions that *were* extracted, the fixed algebraic and
oracle configurations completely recovered **none**. They did produce proved
partial reductions, the largest being 305 AST nodes down to 79 — a 74%
reduction that is still six times the size of the twelve-node source formula.

So: local success, no composition. That is the honest shape of the result, and
it is why the course does not describe expression simplification as a solved
problem.
