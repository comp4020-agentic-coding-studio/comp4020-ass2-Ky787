Each site is three instructions: reload the argument, add the immediate, store
the slot. The constant you wrote is sitting in the instruction stream in
little-endian order — `05 01 01 01 01` is `add eax, 0x01010101` — and that is
the first useful fact about machine code for an analyst. Constants are
landmarks. They are how you find the same computation again after someone has
rearranged everything around it.

:::warning[A landmark is not a proof]
Finding `0x12345678` in a binary tells you where to look. It does not tell you
that the operation around it is still the one the source wrote, and from week 3
onward it frequently will not be.
:::

## The counts do not agree, and that is the point

The same target function, measured three ways in the retained matrix:

<Numbers
  items={[
    { value: "155", label: "LLVM IR instructions in the clean target", source: "showcase_matrix_v1 — O0 comparison table" },
    { value: "115", label: "x64 machine instructions in the same function", source: "showcase_matrix_v1 — O0 comparison table" },
    { value: "467", label: "bytes of machine code, prologue to return", source: "showcase_matrix_v1 — O0 comparison table" },
    { value: "51", label: "machine instructions for the same function at O1", sourceId: "assets/reports/optimization_survival.md" },
  ]}
/>

One function; four numbers, all correct. The IR count is higher than the
machine count because IR spells out addressing that one x64 instruction does
for free. The O1 count is less than half the O0 count because at `-O0` the
compiler reloads the argument from the stack for every single site and at `-O1`
it stops.

This is the measurement discipline the rest of the course depends on. Whenever
you see a number about a program, three questions come before believing it:
which representation, which convention, and against which comparator at which
optimisation level.

## Where an obfuscator can get in

Four places, and the choice determines what happens to the transformation
afterwards.

<LectureTable id="01_transformation_layers" class="sc-wide" />

The last column is the one to hold on to. A source-level transformation has to
survive the entire compiler after it; an IR-level transformation has to survive
instruction selection; a post-link transformation has no optimiser after it at
all. In week 5 we will find that this difference decides whether some
transformations exist in the final binary.

</section>

Use the left pane. Find the eight `add` instructions, find the eight
`xor 12345678h`, and notice that they are as regular in the disassembler as
they were in the source. A clean `-O0` build is nearly a transliteration of the
C, which is exactly why it makes a usable comparator and a poor model of real
software.

## Studio: find a constant, then follow its meaning

Working from the four files below, and nothing else:

1. Pick one of the eight XOR sites. Locate it in the C, in the IR, and in the
   disassembly. Write down the line or address of each.
2. Say what the compiler added around it that the author did not write, and why
   each addition is there.
3. `demo_substitution` contains more than twenty-four operations. Twenty-four
   of them are the repeated sites the corpus was designed around; the rest are
   setup and the final reduction. Mark which is which, and say how you decided.
4. One sentence, to be repeated all semester: *what would have to be true for
   my answer to step 1 to be wrong?*

Bring the answers to step 3 and step 4. Step 3 is where people disagree, and
the disagreement is the useful part — the corpus documentation says "twenty-four
sites" and does not mean "twenty-four instructions".
