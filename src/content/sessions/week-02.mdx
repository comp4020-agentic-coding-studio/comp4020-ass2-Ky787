A working definition, and then five hours of trouble with it.

> **Obfuscation** is a transformation applied to a program that is intended to
> preserve its observable behaviour while making some specific analysis of it
> more expensive.

Everything interesting is in the two vague words. *Which* analysis, and
*preserve* according to whom. This week is about turning both into questions
with answers you can check.

<section class="sc-section">

## Which analysis, and at which representation

An obfuscator does not make a program "harder". It makes one particular way of
looking at the program harder, and it does that at one particular point in the
pipeline. Week 1 gave you the four points. Here is what each choice commits the
tool to:

<LectureTable id="01_transformation_layers" class="sc-wide" />

The consequence is the reason week 5 exists. A transformation applied to C
source has to get past the entire native compiler. One applied to LLVM IR has
to get past instruction selection. One applied to a linked binary has nothing
after it — no optimiser will undo it, and no optimiser will fix it either.

</section>

Sixty-eight planned build conditions were produced from the frozen source.

<section class="sc-section">

## The lane that does not run

:::error[Static evidence only]
BinProtect applied all four of its transformations to both an `-O0` and an
`-O2` input and returned exit code 0 for all eight invocations. Not one of the
eight protected executables is valid at runtime: seven terminate with access
violation `0xC0000005`, and the eighth produced no output and was killed after
fifteen seconds. Every protected file's stdout is empty. The two clean inputs
printed all six values and exited 0.

Nothing on this site presents a BinProtect protected binary as a working
sample, and none is published here. Its transformations are real and visible in
the output files; its outputs are evidence about structure and about nothing
else.
:::

This is the first of several places where the course's habit of separating
outcomes earns its keep. "The tool exited zero and its transformations are
plainly visible in the output" and "the tool produced a working protected
program" are different claims, and here the first is true and the second is
false eight times out of eight.

It is also a warning about reading a tool's own description of itself. Upstream
capability descriptions do not override a local runtime failure, and a local
runtime failure on one program does not prove the tool never works. Both halves
of that are needed.

</section>

<section class="sc-section">

## Targeting: what the tool was told to transform

You would like to say "the obfuscator transformed *this function*". For two of
the four compilers tested, that turned out not to be available.

Annotation-only pilots were run first: mark one function with
`__attribute__((annotate("sub")))`, build, and check that only it changed. On
the tested OLLVM-16 and Hikari builds, the annotated target compiled *and was
not transformed*. In OLLVM-16's case the legacy annotation reader did not
recognise the opaque-pointer representation this front end emits. The retained
matrix therefore uses global switches — `-mllvm -sub`, `-enable-subobf` and
their siblings — which transform everything eligible in the module.

Polaris's selective targeting did work, with suffix annotations paired to its
pass pipelines.

The measurement consequence is precise and small. Target-function measurements
stay isolated, because they are taken from the named function's own bytes. But
other functions in the same binary may also have been transformed, so nothing
here supports a claim about *function-isolated treatment*, and nothing here
proves annotation support is impossible in some other build of these
compilers.

  The same function before and after one arithmetic transformation, at a zoom
  where no instruction is readable. Both are a *single basic block* — the
  control flow is identical and all the growth is inside one straight line of
  code. Nothing here ranks one tool against another, and nothing here is a
  measurement: the retained instruction counts come from the disassembly, not
  from the height of a rectangle.

Keep this image in mind for two reasons. It is the honest reason obfuscation
works — someone has to read that — and it is a trap, because block count and
expression size are independent, and in week 6 you will meet a transformation
that explodes the graph while barely touching the arithmetic.

</section>
