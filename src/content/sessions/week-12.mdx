## What transferred

Three things crossed an implementation boundary in this corpus.

**The branch proof plus a separate patcher.** A generic symbolic harness proved
thirty-eight injected gates one-sided across two unrelated obfuscators, while
leaving sixteen genuine conditions two-sided, with no false positives. A
separate program turned those proofs into changed bytes. 9,189 differential
triples, zero mismatches.

**Local arithmetic recovery.** Ten of twelve isolated sites recovered across
three different implementations, every accepted candidate carrying an
independent 32-bit equivalence proof, and the two that could not be proved
recorded as UNKNOWN rather than accepted.

**Stock unflattening, within a lineage.** From one LLVM-family implementation to
a closely related one: 25/25 and 28/28 successor pairs, two working
executables.

Look at what those three share. Each one carries an **explicit model** — of
memory and path prefix, of bit width, of dispatcher and state layout — and each
one has a **separate validation step** that could have said no. In the case of
the third, on a fourth specimen, it did.

## What did not

- **A seed is not a reconstruction key.** Two fresh runs of the same command
  with the same RNG seed produced IR with different hashes for one pass. The
  exact retained artefact is the specimen, not the command that made it.
- **Local recovery is not whole-function recovery.** Ten of twelve isolated
  sites; zero of twenty composed return expressions.
- **An `-O0` result is not an `-O2` result.** Two bogus-control-flow lanes are
  indistinguishable from clean by `-O1`. One unflattening success does not
  survive its own implementation's `-O2`.
- **A mathematical identity is not a machine identity.** Not until you have
  checked it at the width the machine uses.
- **An initialised-state invariant is not a claim about arbitrary state.** Zero
  of three gates from generic entry state; three of three from one captured
  state.
- **A discovery failure is not a solver failure.** Six blocks found where
  thirty-eight exist, before any analysis ran.

The pattern is uncomfortable and it is the course's thesis: the methods that
travelled were the ones that modelled semantics and validated separately. The
methods that did not travel were the ones that recognised a shape.

## A protocol, not a checklist

Given a target function and no information about what was done to it, the order
below is what this corpus supports. It is a protocol because each step names
what it assumes, and you get to say so out loud.

1. **Get a matched comparator.** Same compiler family, same optimisation level.
   Without it, every count you take is unanchored. Most of the bad claims in
   this field die here.
2. **Decide what is being hidden.** Expression complexity, branch outcome, or
   successor relation? Those need different methods and produce different
   outputs. Look at block count against expression size: a one-block function
   with 678 instructions is not the same problem as a 60-block dispatcher.
3. **Establish ground truth where you can.** A clean build, a source file, a
   canonical output, a test vector. If ground truth exists, use it, and say
   that you had it — a result checked against known answers is worth more than
   one that could not be.
4. **Choose the method by the representation, not the vendor.** Expression
   recovery for arithmetic, symbolic branch proof for gates, successor recovery
   for dispatchers, lifting when you want the whole function in one
   representation. Naming the obfuscator buys nothing; in this corpus one
   filename was wrong about which binary it even showed.
5. **Write the assumption down before you run anything.** Which values are
   symbolic. What the memory model covers. What bit width. Which path prefix.
   If you cannot write it, you will not be able to report the result.
6. **Validate at the level of the claim.** An expression claim needs an
   equivalence proof at the right width. A CFG claim needs a convention and a
   comparator in the same convention. A lifted-function claim needs test
   vectors. An executable claim needs the executable run.
7. **Write down what you could not establish.** Not as a disclaimer. As the
   part of the result that tells the next person where to start.

## Two places this course stops

**The lane that never ran.** One post-link tool applied four transformations to
two inputs and returned exit code 0 for all eight invocations. Seven of the
eight protected executables terminate with an access violation and the eighth
hangs. Its transformations are visible and real; its outputs support static
observations and nothing else. There is no end-to-end deobfuscation lane there
because there was never a working protected program to deobfuscate.

**Virtualization.** Translating protected behaviour into custom bytecode run by
an embedded interpreter is where a lot of commercial protection actually lives,
and this corpus contains **no** virtualization or devirtualization experiment.
It was intentionally not tested. So the course can tell you what the problem is
and cannot tell you anything measured about it, and week 12 is not going to
pretend otherwise by reasoning from the results above. If you want to work on
it, that is a research question with an empty results section, which is the
best kind.

## Studio: your capstone protocol, out loud

Bring your sample and whatever you have. Twenty minutes each, in pairs, then a
round of the whole room.

1. State what you think is being hidden in your function, and the evidence for
   that. One minute.
2. Name the two representations you will work in and why those two.
3. For each method you plan to use, state the assumption it depends on. Your
   partner's job is to find the assumption you left out.
4. For each conclusion you expect to reach, name the level you will validate it
   at and how.
5. Read your limitations section aloud. This is the part people skip and the
   part markers read first, and hearing your own draft is the fastest way to
   notice that it says nothing.

Then one question to the room, which is the question the course has been
building towards for twelve weeks: **what would make you abandon your current
conclusion?** If there is no answer, there is no conclusion yet.
