## The brief

> Here is a binary and a target function. Nobody is going to tell you what was
> done to it. Find out as much as you can defend, and be exact about where your
> defence stops.

This is not "implement a deobfuscator". Nobody writes a deobfuscator in three
weeks, and the course has spent twelve weeks showing you that the hard part is
not the implementation. The deliverable is a justified analysis.

## What you are given

One sample from the course corpus, with its treatment label and its result
files withheld, prepared as a teaching derivative. What comes with it:

- the executable and the target function's export name and RVA;
- a matched clean comparator from the same compiler family at the same
  optimisation level;
- the frozen `showcase.c`, so ground truth for the *source* is available even
  though the treatment is not;
- lab access to the analysis tools used in the course.

Two things follow from that design, and you should plan around both. Ground
truth exists, so a conclusion can be checked and a wrong one will be found.
And the comparator is matched, so "it is bigger than the clean one" is
available to you immediately and is worth nothing on its own.

Some samples in the pool have a known failure in them. If your sample is one
of those, finding and localising the failure is the strongest result available
to you — and reporting a success that is not there is the weakest.

## What you submit

**A report**, ten to fifteen pages, and **a working directory** containing the
files a marker needs to follow it — extracted expressions, graphs, solver
queries and their outputs, test vectors, scripts. The report is what is marked;
the directory is what makes it checkable.

The report needs these parts, in whatever order serves the argument:

- **Provenance.** Every file you touched, with its SHA-256. If you produced a
  file, say what produced it, at what version, with what flags.
- **What you chose to do, and why.** Not a tool list. For each method: the
  question it was chosen to answer, and the assumption it depends on. "Symbolic
  execution, because the branch outcome may be input-independent, which assumes
  the memory model covers the globals the predicate reads."
- **The analysis.** At least two representations. Most good reports use three.
- **Validation.** For each conclusion: what level it was validated at, and how.
  An expression proved equivalent under a stated bit width. A successor
  relation checked against source regions. A rewritten binary executed against
  test vectors — with the vectors, and the count.
- **Limitations.** What you could not establish, what you tried that failed,
  and what evidence would settle each open question. Negative results with
  their method stated are worth marks here; silence is not.

## How it is marked

Look at the weights. Validation is worth as much as the analysis, and
limitations are worth more than communication.

That is the course's whole argument turned into a rubric. Two of the results
you have studied — a rewrite whose graph shrank from 56 blocks to 10 in one
consistent Miasm convention and returns the wrong value, and a lift whose IR
verifies and miscomputes 3,917 of 4,118 inputs — would each have passed any
review that marked only the analysis and the output. Both were caught by the validation step, and only by the
validation step.

So a report that concludes less and validates what it concludes will outscore a
report that concludes more. Concretely:

- An unvalidated claim scores nothing under *validation*, and it also costs
  marks under *limitations*, because a report that does not know which of its
  claims are unvalidated has not understood its own evidence.
- "The rewritten binary is correct" requires a runtime record. Not a valid PE
  header, not a clean graph, not a verifier-clean lift.
- A cross-tool comparison needs a matched comparator at a matched optimisation
  level, or it is not a comparison.
- Naming the obfuscator earns nothing. It is not an analysis result, and in one
  of the course's specimens the filename was wrong about which binary it
  showed.

A strong capstone says, for every conclusion, which of those four it has
reached — and notices when it has reached the third without the fourth.

## Scope and timing

Three weeks of work, alongside weeks 10 to 12. The realistic scope for that
time is **one target function analysed properly**, not a whole binary surveyed.
Reports that try to cover every export arrive thin, and thinness shows up
under validation first.

Start with the comparator diff in week 10, choose your methods in week 11, and
spend week 12 on validation and the limitations section. The limitations
section is the one markers read first, so write it early and keep adding to it
rather than composing it the night before.

## Extensions and integrity

Extensions through the convenor before the deadline. The sample is individual.
Discussing method choice with others is fine and should be declared; handing
over a recovered expression or a patch is not. See
[course information](/policies/).
