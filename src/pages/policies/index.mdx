## Who this is for

Upper-level undergraduate or introductory postgraduate, in systems or security.
You do not need prior experience of symbolic program analysis; SSA, SMT,
symbolic execution and lifting are all introduced in the course. You do need to
arrive able to read code.

**Assumed before week 1:**

- C functions, pointers, arrays, control flow, and unsigned integer arithmetic;
- binary and hexadecimal notation, bitwise operators, and basic modular
  arithmetic;
- introductory architecture: registers, memory, the stack, calls, and
  conditional branches;
- comfort at a command line, and enough Python to read a short script. You will
  not be asked to write one.

**Explicitly not assumed:** compiler implementation experience, theorem
proving, or any prior reverse engineering.

If you can read `showcase.c` and predict what `demo_substitution(0x1337)`
returns, you are ready. If you cannot, week 1's studio is where to say so.

## Teaching pattern

Twelve two-hour studios, one per week, on Wednesdays. Four spine lectures, in
weeks 1, 4, 7 and 10, each opening a block of three weeks.

Studios are where the work happens: you read the evidence, disagree about what
it shows, and leave having written down one thing you could not conclude.
Attendance is not marked. It is also not optional in any practical sense —
reading someone else's notes about a disassembly listing transfers almost
nothing.

There is a two-week teaching break after week 6. A2 and the capstone are both
set before it, deliberately, so that the break is usable.

## The lab, and the rule about running things

The course provides a Windows x64 image containing the twenty-one retained
specimens, a disassembler, and the analysis tools used in the studios.

**Every core exercise has a read-only route.** Source, LLVM IR, generated C,
exact disassembly, recovered control-flow graphs, solver queries and result
files are all published on this site and are sufficient to complete the
studios, A1 and A2 on any platform, offline, with a text editor. No paid
decompiler and no commercial binary-analysis platform is required for anything
that carries marks.

Execution and debugging need the lab image. The rules there are short:

1. **Run only what the [evidence page](/evidence/) marks runnable.** One
   specimen is an explicitly incorrect rewrite, kept as negative evidence. It
   is for inspection.
2. **Run them in the lab image, not on your own machine.** These are obfuscated
   Windows binaries. Endpoint protection will sometimes take an interest in
   them, and that is the correct behaviour.
3. **If a launch is blocked, it stays blocked.** Do not disable or work around
   endpoint protection. A blocked launch is not permission to bypass anything;
   use the read-only route instead.
4. **Decline the original PDB when a debugger offers it for a rewritten
   specimen.** The identifier survives patching; the instruction mappings
   inside a rewritten function do not.
5. **Navigate by export name or by loaded base plus RVA.** No PDB or MAP file
   is supplied, on purpose, and address-space layout randomisation will move
   preferred addresses.

Nothing on this site is a distribution channel for executables, and the corpus
is not licensed for redistribution outside the course.

## Legal and ethical scope

This is a course in program analysis, taught on a 483-line demonstration
program written for the purpose. It is not a course in defeating software
protection you do not own, and the skills in it do not come with permission to
use them on anything.

Two practical lines:

- Analyse only software you own, software licensed to you for that purpose, or
  the course corpus.
- The corpus contains no malware, and nothing in the course requires a sample
  that might.

## Assessment

Three pieces — 25%, 30% and 45% — and no exam. The briefs, the spec lines and
the marking criteria are on the [assessment page](/assessments/).

One rule is worth stating here because it is the course's own standard turned
on your submission rather than boilerplate: **name any tool you used and its
version, and treat its output as a claim you now have to support.** Every
claim cites a file and a line or an address. Two of the results you will study
are on this site because somebody applied that standard and somebody else had
not.

## Accessibility

The course's evidence is mostly text, which helps. Two known problems and what
is done about them:

- **Control-flow graphs.** Several are several metres wide at natural size.
  Every one on this site is published as SVG — so it scales and its text is
  real text — with a described caption, and the numbers quoted beside it come
  from the result files rather than from the picture. Where a graph carries the
  argument, the argument is also in prose.
- **Disassembler screenshots.** These are raster images of somebody else's tool
  and cannot be made into text. Each has alt text describing what is visible,
  each can be enlarged in place or opened at full resolution, and — this is the
  important part — no claim on this site rests on one. They illustrate; the
  result files establish.

Colour is never the only signal: every verdict on this site carries a word
(PASS, WRONG, UNSAT, STATIC ONLY) and a shape as well as a colour.
